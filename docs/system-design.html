System design · v1

Sahayak roadside dispatch

A stranded vehicle owner asks for help. The system offers the job to the best available mechanic, tow or fuel partner within reach, scored on more than distance, then tracks the job until it is paid.

As of 2026-11-17Decisions: ADR-001 to ADR-032 (docs/adr/ADR.md)Measurements: docs/load-test-dispatch-concurrency.md

1Requirements and targets

Functional

  • Owner registers by phone OTP, adds vehicles, requests a service with a GPS pin and sees an upfront price.
  • The system offers the job to one partner at a time, best score first. If nobody is found, it widens the search over ten minutes.
  • Partner accepts or rejects, travels, enters the owner's start code, completes, and the job is settled (UPI or cash).
  • Both sides track status. Location is shared live, contact details only after assignment, and owners can send a public trip link to someone they trust.
  • Staff run a live jobs board, verify partners, reassign jobs, edit prices and handle disputes. Every staff action is audited.

Invariants that are not negotiable: every status change writes a job_status_history row, identity comes from the verified JWT and never from the request body, and a partner who has only been offered a job gets no owner contact (ADR-017).

2Architecture

A single FastAPI service, layered route → service → repository. Durable state lives in Postgres with PostGIS. Volatile partner locations live only in Redis GEO. Supabase issues the JWTs and the backend only verifies them (ADR-001, 002).

3Data model

19 tables. The design rests on three of them: a job is a request, an assignment is one dispatch attempt, and history is append-only (ADR-004).

erDiagram
  users ||--o{ vehicles : owns
  users ||--o{ jobs : requests
  vehicles ||--o{ jobs : "for"
  services ||--o{ jobs : "priced from"
  jobs ||--o{ job_assignments : "offered via"
  partners ||--o{ job_assignments : receives
  jobs ||--o{ job_status_history : "every change"
  jobs ||--o| payments : settles
  jobs ||--o{ ratings : "both ways"
  jobs ||--o{ job_disputes : "reported on"
  partners ||--o{ partner_services : offers
  partners ||--o{ partner_equipment : "verified kit"
  • Derived data is never stored. A partner's active job count is computed from job_assignments joined to jobs.status at read time (ADR-008).
  • A partner's rating is recomputed from source by the service and Bayesian-smoothed: an unrated partner scores 3.5 / 5 (ADR-009, 018).
  • A job keeps the price it was created with even after staff change the catalogue price (ADR-028).
  • Deleting an account anonymises the row so job history stays intact (ADR-025). Staff actions go to an append-only audit log in the same transaction (ADR-024).

4Dispatch

Candidates come from Redis (radius search). SQL filters them on verification, service skill, required equipment and capacity. Survivors are ranked by a weighted score:

TermWeightSignal
Distance0.4Normalised to the radius actually searched (ADR-023)
Load0.2Active jobs right now. Full capacity is a filter, not a score (ADR-009)
Skill0.2Match between the service and what the partner offers
Rating0.2Bayesian-smoothed. In a controlled test it changed the chosen partner in 70 % of jobs, against 0 % when blinded (§4.2 of the load-test report)

Why a lock is never held across Redis: a row lock held during a network call turns one slow dependency into a queue of blocked writers. The search runs unlocked. The job row is then locked and re-checked before writing, so an owner's cancel during the search always wins (ADR-015, 023).

5Job lifecycle

Partner-driven moves are guarded by ALLOWED_TRANSITIONS in the job service. Moves made by dispatch, the owner and staff have their own routes and their own ADRs. Every arrow writes a history row.

no_match_found is not final while the ten-minute window is open. A rescued job counts as matched in analytics, and its history still shows the miss.

The model

The scarce resource is connection-seconds per second. With 15 shared connections the budget is 15, and staying under 70 % of it leaves room for queueing. Every request spends some of that budget, so the model adds up what each kind of user spends:

  • Job creation holds a connection for 1.198 s (measured).
  • A polled read is estimated at 3 round trips (BEGIN, SELECT, ROLLBACK) × RTT. estimate, not measured
  • An online, idle partner polls offers every 15 s, notifications every 30 s and sends location every 60 s (mobile and web intervals in the code), ≈ 0.12 requests/s.
  • An active job: the owner tracks every 5 s, the partner's job screen polls every 20 s, and both badges poll every 30 s, ≈ 0.32 requests/s.

What the model shows: at pilot volumes the budget goes to idle partners polling, not to dispatch. 300 partners and 60 live jobs spend about a third of the budget, and job creation is under 1 % of it. Polling grows with the number of online partners, so it hits the ceiling first.

So the 1.2 s dispatch hold time is a latency problem: the stranded owner waits for an offer, and a burst of requests queues behind it. Push and server-sent updates are the capacity fix. Both are in the roadmap.
